Objection 01 Upheld Counter-review §3A — keep Spotter as a mnemonic, not as the promise

"SPTR — short for Spotter" is a better answer than either previous position

The panel said keep SPTR, drop the lockup, pair the mark with a functional descriptor. The counter-review's version is sharper: separate the origin of the name from the promise of the product. A mnemonic explains a spelling; a lockup makes a claim. Only the second one collides with six competitors and with the physical-safety problem. Adopted.

This resolves what looked like a disagreement and was really a missing distinction. The operator's actual objective — "SPTR should be remembered as Spotter" — survives intact, and nothing risky comes with it.

One condition the counter-review missed

A mnemonic only works in a language where the word means what you intend. In Russian, споттер is predominantly an auto-body spot-welding tool; the lifting role is named with a verb (подстрахуешь?), and страховка means an insurance policy. "SPTR — сокращение от Spotter" explains a Latin abbreviation with an English word that means a different object.

Ruling: the mnemonic is approved for English and refused for Russian. In Russian the mark stands alone and the page earns the name through the mechanic instead. This is not a translation task — it is a decision about which market gets the wordplay, and it has to be made before a wordmark is drawn.

Objection 02 Rejected Counter-review §3B — the panel claimed "Digital Spotter" itself makes SPTR a medical device

The panel report does not make that claim

This objection argues against a categorical legal conclusion. The published report attaches the regulatory risk to injury claims specifically, and says so in the same sentence:

"Copy like trains around your injuries can convert a wellness app into an unregistered medical device placed on the EU market." Panel report, §14 — the only place the medical-device line is drawn

And on classification, the report's instruction is exactly the counter-review's: decide it with counsel, not by inference. On the name it says "needs counsel, not another search"; on the brand it never cites MDR at all — its three grounds for dropping the lockup are store crowding, Russian failure, and the 16+ audience.

Ruling: rejected as addressed to a position not held. On the substance there is no dispute: injury-treatment and injury-compensation wording carries the exposure, the metaphor alone does not, and only a regulatory professional can classify the product. All three were already the panel's position.

Worth stating plainly, because it matters for how the operator reads both documents: the disagreement here is about wording in a review, not about what to do. Nothing in the action list changes.

Objection 03 Split — settled at source Counter-review §3C — "required verbatim" disclaimer is not established

Half of it is mandated word-for-word; the other half was ours, not the regulator's

This was the one genuine factual dispute in the counter-review, so it was checked against the policy text rather than argued.

ElementStatus in Play policyRuling

Ruling: the label "required, verbatim" was correct for one quoted sentence and wrong for the paragraph that surrounded it. The counter-review is right that a reviewer must not stamp composed wording as regulator-mandated, and that correction stands. The panel is right that the quoted phrase is prescribed and cannot be paraphrased.

Practical consequence: keep the quoted sentence exactly as it appears in policy, treat everything else in that footer as draft copy for counsel, and drop the word "verbatim" from it.

Objection 04 Upheld Counter-review §3D — six blockers before design conflates release with design

The taxonomy was wrong and the counter-review's is better

A missing signing key and a wrong launcher label do not stop anyone drawing a page. Bundling them into "not ready for design" answered a narrower question than it appeared to — and the operator's brief did ask design and implementation as one question, which is how they got merged. That is an explanation, not a defence. The three-tier split is adopted.

One item moves back — and by the counter-review's own argument

It places trademark clearance under "blocks public beta" and lists "absence of a name decision" as not blocking visual design, while separately advising against committing "major brand spend" before clearance. Both cannot hold: a wordmark is the first thing a designer draws and the most expensive thing to redraw. Drawing an identity for a name that may not be usable is the brand spend the counter-review warns against.

Ruling: name clearance blocks visual design. Everything else it moved out stays out.

Objection 05 Partly upheld Counter-review §3E — "Russian-first" is an evidence-free mandate

Right that it is a business decision; wrong that there is no evidence

Upheld on form: the panel stated a marketing-language decision as a finding, and acquisition language belongs to the operator, not to a code review. The word "must" was not earned.

Refused on the premise that there is nothing behind it. There is, and it is stronger than the funnel arithmetic the panel actually cited — it is in the code:

"Order is load-bearing: this list is passed to MaterialApp.supportedLocales verbatim, and Flutter falls back to the FIRST entry for any device locale it cannot match." mobile/lib/core/settings/app_settings.dart — above kSupportedLocaleCodes = ['ru', 'en']

Russian is not merely available. It is the fallback for every device locale the app cannot match, which makes it the product's default in the only sense that reaches a user. That is a repository fact, and it produces the finding in the next section.

Ruling: the recommendation is downgraded to a recommendation. Russian remains the panel's advice on the evidence available; the decision is the operator's, and the design system must fit both languages regardless of how it goes.

Objection 06 Rejected Counter-review §3F — the panel made refusal the hero message

The panel's architecture already puts recognition in the hero and refusal in position four

The report's page architecture opens with "Hero fused with the recognition demo — the mechanic is the value proposition", and places the refusal section fourth, with a stated reason for the position: a disclosed negative helps only when it follows the positive material. The counter-review notes that the panel "itself effectively admits this by still putting machine recognition in the hero" — which is not an admission, it is the recommendation.

Ruling: rejected as addressed to a position not held. The confusion is traceable to one thing the panel did do: it ranked refusal first in feature priority — meaning most defensible — while placing recognition first in page order. Two different rankings, and the report should have named the difference instead of leaving a reader to reconcile them.

What is adopted: the counter-review's vocabulary. Acquisition job versus trust differentiator is a cleaner distinction than "priority" and "order", and it removes the ambiguity permanently. Use it.

Objection 07 Rejected Counter-review §7 — keep injury filtering off the v1 page entirely

This is the substantive disagreement, and the counter-review does not follow it to its conclusion

Three of its recommendations are each individually defensible. Together they empty the page.

  1. It agrees machine recognition is not defensible — four to five apps ship it, one on Play with the same catalog pitch.
  2. It demotes the refusal behaviour from lead message to trust layer.
  3. It removes injury filtering from marketing altogether until coverage and clinical review mature.

Apply all three and what remains above the fold is: photograph a machine, get exercises, from a large library. That is the description of GymScan, Gymeo, GymVision and "Spotter — Your Gym Reference". The page would be a late fourth entrant describing a commodity — the exact outcome the counter-review agrees must be avoided.

The reason given for removal is real: nine body-region tags, no clinician review, 360 exercises that pass unfiltered. But that argues against a safety claim, not against describing the feature. The distinction has already been drawn and cleared:

"You tell SPTR what to avoid; it takes those exercises off the list. That is a shorter list, not a medical clearance." Panel claim matrix — the approved wording, which makes no health claim of any kind

That sentence promises an input and a mechanism. It does not promise safety, prevention, clearance or clinical judgement. Removing it removes the only differentiating capability the product can honestly show today — and it removes it on grounds that the wording already satisfies.

Ruling: rejected. The feature stays on the page, in the approved wording, with the complement published (360 untagged). If a claim can be made truthfully and it is the only differentiator available, deleting it is not caution — it is choosing to compete on the commodity instead.

Four things both reviews missed

1. The app can open in Russian for a tester recruited in English

Because Russian is the first entry in the supported-locale list, any device whose locale the app cannot match resolves to Russian — a German, French or Polish phone included. A tester recruited through an English page, who then sideloads and opens a Russian interface, is a trust break at the same step as the wrong launcher label, and for the same reason: it happens after the risky install, when confidence is thinnest.

Severity: blocks public beta. Either the fallback becomes English, or the English page tells testers the app may open in Russian and where the language switch is. This also settles the language question's urgency — it is not deferrable to "campaign evidence", because the shipped default already answers it.

2. Stripe is in sandbox — now near-certain, from the code itself

Earlier rounds could not determine live-versus-test because the key is a managed secret. A settings comment states it directly: "Stripe is in sandbox, sandbox only accepts its own test card numbers", written to explain why a tier-override switch exists for testing paid surfaces. Still a comment rather than a deployment check, but authored by the person who hit the problem. No pricing may appear on the page.

3. Both rounds independently wrote nearly the same headline

The panel: "The machine you always walk past — find out what it's for." The counter-review: "That machine you always skip? Start there." Two reviews with different briefs converged on the same insight, which is the strongest signal either document produces about a line. That insight — a specific, recognisable moment of avoidance — is the hero. The remaining choice is phrasing.

4. "Start there" is the one line in the counter-review its own framework would block

It instructs a novice to begin on a machine they do not know. Every other proposal in that document is careful about exactly this. Minor, and easily fixed by keeping the question and dropping the instruction — but it shows the value of the rule the counter-review itself proposes: check the finalists against the safety list before publication, including your own.

Separately, its claim that multiple products already use "digital spotter" as a descriptor was not corroborated by a check. Unverified rather than wrong — but it does not carry weight in the naming decision as things stand.
